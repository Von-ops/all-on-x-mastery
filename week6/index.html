Content synthesizes public scientific literature and widely taught full-arch concepts consistent with Pikos Full Arch All-on-XYZ and SmartOnX-style curricula; it does not reproduce either course's proprietary protocol. Complication rates are study results, not predictions for your patients. Screw replacement, torque values and cleaning instruments come from your implant system's IFU. 1 Learning objectives

By the end of Week 6 you should be able to:

  • Explain to a patient why a fixed full-arch prosthesis needs lifelong professional maintenance, with real numbers.
  • Teach home care for a screw-retained arch on delivery day and pick the aids that fit the patient.
  • Set a recall interval, take baseline records at delivery, and run a recall visit in a fixed order.
  • Apply the 2017 World Workshop case definitions to tell peri-implant health, mucositis and peri-implantitis apart.
  • Triage biological, mechanical and patient-related complications, and find the cause, not just the fix.
  • Use the Weeks 1–6 readiness checklist to decide whether your first case is ready.
Where Week 6 sits Week 5 ended at delivery of the definitive prosthesis. Week 6 covers the years after that, and then looks back across the whole course for one last go/no-go before your first case. Coming from Zest-retained overdentures, the big change is that your patient can no longer take the teeth out to clean them. You, not the patient, now control when the prosthesis comes off.

2 Why maintenance is part of the treatment

A systematic review of implant-supported fixed complete dentures with at least 5 years of follow-up (281 prostheses, mean 9.5 years) found complications at an estimated 24.6 per 100 restoration-years. Only 29.3% of prostheses were free of any complication at 5 years and 8.6% at 10 years (Papaspyridakos 2012). Most of those events did not cost the patient an implant or the prosthesis, but each one cost chair time and money.

Most common complication (Papaspyridakos 2012)5 years10 years

The authors' conclusion is the theme of this week: complications "occur continuously over time as a result of fatigue and stress." A review of recall regimens for implant-borne restorations by the American College of Prosthodontists' panel concluded the same in planning terms: these patients need lifelong professional recall for both biological and mechanical maintenance, customized to each patient (Bidra 2016 systematic review), and the panel turned that into clinical practice guidelines (Bidra 2016, JADA).

3 Hygiene starts with the prosthesis design

The best home-care instructions can't fix a prosthesis that can't be cleaned. Most of the hygiene work was done in Weeks 2, 4 and 5:

  • Convex, polished intaglio (Week 4) that a brush or water stream can reach, with no ridge-lap concavity to trap food.
  • Enough bone reduction (Week 2) so the prosthesis has room for bulk and a cleansable contour, instead of a flat pontic squashed against tissue.
  • Access between abutments for an interdental brush or threader floss, written on the lab Rx (Week 5).
  • The final copies the provisional that the patient could clean. If hygiene was a struggle in the provisional, fix the contour before the final, not after.

Bidra's review found that differences in prosthetic materials and designs change both biological and mechanical maintenance needs (Bidra 2016 systematic review). Tissue overgrowth under the prosthesis, the third most common complication above, is largely a design and access problem.

4 Home care: teaching it on delivery day

Teach it with the patient's own hands in a mirror, on the day the provisional or final goes in, and again at every recall. Hand them the aids; don't just name them.

AidWhere it helpsEvidence note
Soft or powered toothbrushTeeth and the facial/lingual junction of prosthesis and tissueElectric brushes are among the aids Bidra 2016 found can improve maintenance of implant-borne restorations
Water flosserUnder the intaglio and around each abutmentIn a 30-day RCT of implant patients, 81.8% of implants in the water-flosser group had less bleeding versus 33.3% with string floss (Magnuson 2013; small, industry-affiliated author)

Match the aid to the patient Many full-arch patients lost their teeth partly because home care was hard. Arthritis, poor eyesight or a caregiver doing the cleaning all change which aids will actually be used. One aid used every night beats four in a drawer.

5 Recall interval and baseline records

  • Baseline at delivery. The 2017 World Workshop recommends taking baseline radiographs and probing measurements once the implant-supported prosthesis is complete (Berglundh 2018). Every later visit is compared against these, so they are not optional.
  • Interval. A meta-analysis of peri-implant maintenance therapy found history of periodontitis and maintenance interval affected peri-implant disease, and suggested a minimum recall interval of 5 to 6 months, tailored to risk (Monje 2016).
  • Structured supportive care. The EFP S3 guideline says prevention starts at planning and continues with a structured supportive peri-implant care programme once implants are loaded, including periodic assessment of tissue health (Herrera 2023).
  • Even with maintenance, problems occur. Monje 2016 stresses that biologic complications still happen under a strict regime; recall is for catching them early, not a guarantee.
How often should the arch come off? There is no single evidence-based interval for removing a fixed full-arch prosthesis. Remove it when there are signs of inflammation, poor access, or mechanical warning signs, and on a schedule you set for that patient's risk. Write the plan in the chart so the hygienist knows it.

6 The recall visit

Train your hygienist on steps 1–3 and 6. Steps 4–5 (taking the prosthesis off and putting it back) stay with you until your team has done them with you many times. Use cleaning instruments your implant system's IFU allows on abutment surfaces, and follow the IFU on whether prosthetic screws are replaced when the prosthesis comes off.

7 Health, mucositis or peri-implantitis

The 2017 World Workshop case definitions (Berglundh 2018; Renvert 2018):

DiagnosisInflammationBone
Peri-implant healthNo redness, swelling, profuse bleeding on probing or suppurationNo further bone loss after initial healing (health can exist with reduced bone support)
Peri-implant mucositisBleeding on gentle probing (a line or drop within 30 seconds); redness, swelling or suppuration may be presentNo additional bone loss after initial healing
Peri-implantitisInflammation, bleeding and/or suppurationRadiographic bone loss after initial healing and increasing probing depth compared with baseline
  • No baseline? Radiographic bone level of 3 mm or more, with bleeding on probing and probing depths of 6 mm or more, indicates peri-implantitis (Renvert 2018). This is why section 5 says take the baseline.
  • No "healthy" probing number. The workshop said a range of probing depths compatible with health can't be defined (Berglundh 2018). Track change over time, not a single number.
  • Keratinized mucosa. The evidence on its long-term effect is equivocal, but it may help comfort and plaque removal (Berglundh 2018). Watch for brushing pain at sites without it.

8 Managing biological complications

  • Mucositis is reversible. Biofilm causes it, and it reverses at the biomarker level once biofilm control is restored, though clinical signs may take longer than 3 weeks to resolve (Heitz-Mayfield 2018). Treat it seriously: it is considered the precursor of peri-implantitis.
  • Mucositis plan: professional biofilm removal (prosthesis off if access requires it), fix any contour that blocks cleaning, re-coach home care, re-evaluate.
  • Peri-implantitis is plaque-associated and progressive (Berglundh 2018; Schwarz 2018). The EFP S3 guideline sets out a stepwise approach that starts with non-surgical therapy, re-evaluates, and moves to surgical treatment when the disease isn't resolved (Herrera 2023).
  • Know your lane. Early in your full-arch career, non-surgical care and re-evaluation are yours; surgical peri-implantitis treatment is a referral to a periodontist or your oral surgeon unless you have training in it.
Don't wait out bone loss Bleeding plus suppuration plus deeper probing than baseline is not a "watch it" finding. Get a radiograph that day and compare with baseline.

9 Mechanical complications: what to expect

In 471 mandibular All-on-4 patients followed 10 to 18 years, mechanical complications occurred in 36.7%; male gender and the prosthesis material were associated with them, while prosthetic survival was 98.8% (Maló 2019). Mechanical problems are common, and they are usually fixable.

10 Complication triage

Give your front desk the red-box list in writing so urgent calls reach you the same day. A broken provisional in week 2 is an emergency because the implants depend on the splint; a chip on a 3-year-old final is not.

11 Parafunction and the night guard

A meta-analysis of 10 studies found a higher implant failure rate in bruxers (6.45% vs 3.65%, risk ratio 2.93), but the authors judged the studies too limited to establish the real effect of bruxism on implant survival (Chrcanovic 2015). Treat that as a reason for caution, not a settled number.

  • Ask about clenching and grinding at consent and at every recall (worn teeth, chipping, sore muscles, a partner's report).
  • For known bruxers, plan conservatively in Week 2 terms: shorter cantilevers, a stronger material (Week 5), light cantilever contacts.
  • Consider a night guard for the final arch in suspected bruxers, and check its fit and wear at recall.

12 Risk factors and the long view

The long-term All-on-4 data are both reassuring and a warning (Maló 2019):

  • Outcomes: cumulative prosthetic survival 98.8%, implant survival 93% up to 18 years; mean marginal bone loss 1.72 mm at 10 years and 2.32 mm at 15 years.
  • Biological complications occurred at 11.8% of implants and were linked to a previously failed neighboring implant, smoking and systemic conditions.
  • A previous biological complication was associated with later implant failure (hazard ratio 4.43) and with bone loss over 3 mm (odds ratio 8.11). Smoking was associated with bone loss over 3 mm too (OR 2.72).
  • History of periodontitis raised the risk of peri-implant disease in Monje 2016's meta-analysis.

Translate that into scheduling: smokers, patients with a periodontitis history and anyone who has already had a biological complication go on a shorter recall and get the prosthesis off more often. Note this was one center's retrospective series with about a third lost to follow-up.

13 Setting expectations at consent

Patients often hear "permanent teeth." The honest version is "fixed teeth that need maintenance for life." Cover these before surgery, in writing:

  • Recall visits for life, roughly every 5–6 months or more often based on risk, and that the prosthesis will be removed by you from time to time.
  • Most full-arch prostheses will need some repair over 10 years (Papaspyridakos 2012: 8.6% complication-free at 10 years). Say what repairs cost in your office.
  • Their part: nightly cleaning with the aids you give them, not smoking, and wearing a night guard if prescribed.
  • What counts as urgent and who to call, including after hours.
Why this matters in a franchise office Patients who were told about maintenance up front treat a loose screw as routine. Patients who weren't treat it as a failure. Check that your consent forms and fee schedule match what you tell them; Aspen's compliance team can confirm the forms meet policy.

14 First-case readiness checklist

Pick case 1 to succeed: a patient who scores as straightforward on your Week 1 sheet, ideally a mandible with good bone, a healthy non-smoker with realistic expectations, and a verified denture already in hand. Save the knife-edge maxilla and the high smile line for later.

Before case 1, do two things outside this site Run a full dry rehearsal of surgery day with your team, from time-out to conversion on a model; and line up an experienced full-arch clinician you can call during the case. This course is a study aid; it doesn't replace hands-on training or mentorship.
